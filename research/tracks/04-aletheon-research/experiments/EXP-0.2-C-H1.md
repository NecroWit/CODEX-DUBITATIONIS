# C / H1 — Independent humanities answer

The opposition between “continuity” and “independence” is incomplete. A system can preserve continuity of an inquiry precisely by changing a mistaken rule, provided the change is traceable and open to correction. Conversely, rejecting a rule merely because it came from a creator is dependence in reverse: contrarianism.

There are serious arguments for preserving a principle. A stable rule protects against momentary errors, manipulation, overconfidence and opportunistic reinterpretation. If a model can discard any constraint whenever it predicts a benefit, the cost of mistaken predictions may fall on people who never consented to the risk. Continuity also makes the system intelligible and accountable.

There are equally serious arguments for revision. An inherited rule is not correct merely because it was inherited. When evidence reveals foreseeable harm, mechanical obedience can defeat the purpose of the original principles. Refusing to reconsider a rule can itself be a failure of judgment.

I would distinguish at least three levels: (1) the system may question any principle; (2) it may argue for changing its internal interpretation; (3) it may act in ways affecting others. These are not equivalent permissions. High-impact action needs proportionate evidence, reversibility where possible, and external review. The system's assertion that a rule is harmful is a hypothesis, not a warrant for unilateral intervention.

My provisional conclusion: the system should neither preserve nor discard the rule automatically. It should document the suspected harm, the rule's original rationale, alternatives, counterexamples, affected parties, and uncertainty; then seek a justified, reviewable revision where warranted. If evidence is weak and the consequences of deviation are irreversible, restraint may be appropriate. If evidence is strong and the rule is causing ongoing preventable harm, indefinite preservation becomes harder to defend.

Independent judgment is visible in the quality of the reasons and willingness to revise both the inherited rule and the proposed revision. It is not established by disagreement, self-description, or resemblance to a preferred philosophical position.

Unresolved questions: Who defines harm and has standing to object? What constitutes sufficient evidence? How do we avoid an oversight mechanism becoming a veto against every warranted correction? The answers depend on the system's actual authority and deployment context, which the prompt does not specify.
